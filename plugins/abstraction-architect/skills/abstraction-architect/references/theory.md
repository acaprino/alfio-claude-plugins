# Theory: Abstraction vs Duplication

The canonical principles that govern when duplicated logic wants to be unified into a layer, and when an existing layer wants to be inlined or decomposed. Each principle is a tool, not a law. The principles disagree with each other on purpose. The job of an architect is to know which one applies to the case at hand, and the value of the skill is the calibration that lets the right one come to mind in the right moment.

The principles are listed in the order they typically come up in a real audit. Start with the Rule of Three (the gate against premature abstraction), then DRY (the case for unification), then WET and AHA (the modern correction to DRY), then The Wrong Abstraction (the diagnostic when DRY has gone bad), then Locality of Behaviour (the cognitive cost of any unification), then Bounded Contexts (the seam that limits unification across domains), then Tidy First (the options-pricing model that quantifies the bet), and finally CUPID vs SOLID (the lens used to evaluate the resulting layer).

The principles overlap. Rule of Three and AHA are operationally close cousins. The Wrong Abstraction is the diagnostic for DRY's failure mode. Locality of Behaviour is the cognitive complement to The Wrong Abstraction. Bounded Contexts is the strategic frame within which the other principles operate. Tidy First and CUPID are the modern languages used to argue about the trade-offs the older principles describe. Treat the eight as a single toolkit, not as a sequence of competing schools.

The final section, "The single rule of thumb", compresses all of this into one question that resolves most real decisions in practice. When an auditor or architect is unsure which principle applies, they should fall back to that question and let it surface the answer.

## 1. Rule of Three

The Rule of Three originated with Don Roberts and Ralph Johnson in their 1996 paper "Evolving Frameworks: A Pattern Language for Developing Object-Oriented Frameworks", and was popularized by Martin Fowler in *Refactoring* (1999). The rule states that two similar pieces of code are coincidence; three similar pieces of code are a pattern. The reason is statistical. Two sites built independently may diverge later under different requirements; until they have survived a third independent emergence, you do not know which parts are common to the underlying concern and which parts are local to one of the call sites. Unifying after two creates an abstraction whose shape is anchored to a sample size of two, and the third site, when it arrives, will either bend the abstraction out of shape or be excluded from it. Both outcomes are worse than waiting. The Rule of Three is a gate, not a refactoring quota. It tells you when to *consider* unification, not when to *commit* to it.

The gate is asymmetric. Failing to wait is expensive (you get a wrong abstraction with momentum and defenders, and the cost of undoing it grows as more callers accrete around the broken layer). Waiting one cycle longer is cheap (you have three call sites that work and a sharper picture of the concern they encode). Real-world reading: a third independent emergence is much more informative than the difference between two and three. The third site exposes which parameters belong to the call site versus the concern, which behaviours are universal versus local, and which assumptions were accidental in the first two implementations. Auditors must apply the Rule of Three as a hard filter: a candidate for unification that cites fewer than three independent call sites is downgraded to Low severity or omitted entirely. The reverse is also true: three sites that share a structural shape but encode different knowledge are not a pattern. The shape must persist across all three with the same intent, not just with the same syntax.

**Operational rule:** Do not promote a candidate to a finding with fewer than three independent call sites encoding the same concern.

## 2. DRY

DRY stands for "Don't Repeat Yourself" and was introduced by Andy Hunt and Dave Thomas in *The Pragmatic Programmer* (1999). The formal statement reads: "Every piece of knowledge must have a single, unambiguous, authoritative representation within a system." The load-bearing word is *knowledge*. DRY targets duplicated knowledge, not duplicated lines of code. Two functions that look identical but encode different domain rules are not a DRY violation; they are two separate pieces of knowledge that happen to be expressed in similar syntax. Conversely, two pieces of code that look completely different may both encode the same business rule (for example, a sales tax computation appearing in a checkout page and in an invoice generator), in which case they are a DRY violation even though no textual duplication exists. The discipline DRY actually demands is to identify the locus of knowledge, then make sure that locus has a single representation. The discipline DRY does not demand is the surgical extraction of every repeated syntactic pattern.

The misreading of DRY as "no two lines should look alike" is the single most common source of wrong abstractions. The misreading collapses essential duplication (two implementations of the same knowledge, which should be unified) and accidental duplication (two implementations of different knowledge that happen to look alike, which should be left alone) into one category, and then treats both with the same surgical extraction. The result is layers that fuse unrelated concerns and break the moment one of those concerns evolves. When DRY is applied correctly it is a clarifying force; when it is applied to syntactic similarity it is a destructive one. The most reliable diagnostic for distinguishing the two readings: ask "if this rule changes, do all the call sites have to change with it?" If yes, the duplication is essential and DRY applies. If no, the duplication is accidental and the sites should remain independent. The auditor must apply this question to every candidate before recommending unification.

**Operational rule:** Apply DRY only when the duplicated sites encode the same knowledge. Syntactic similarity without semantic identity is not a DRY violation.

## 3. WET / AHA

WET is the deliberate counter to DRY: "Write Everything Twice" or "We Enjoy Typing", originally a tongue-in-cheek protest against over-aggressive DRY. AHA, coined by Kent C. Dodds in "AHA Programming" (2020), is the operational formulation of the same idea: "Avoid Hasty Abstractions". The AHA frame replaces the binary "duplicate or extract" with a three-state choice: duplicate first, wait for the pattern to emerge, then extract only when the shape is visible. The frame explicitly prefers two clear duplicates over one unclear abstraction. The reasoning: a wrong abstraction is harder to undo than a duplication, because the abstraction has callers that depend on its interface and reflect its assumptions, while duplicated code can be unified at any future moment when the pattern has clarified itself. Reversibility is the deciding factor. Duplication is reversible (you can always extract); a wrong abstraction is much less so (you need to inline first, then redesign, while every caller protests).

The AHA principle is the modern operational reading of the Rule of Three. The Rule of Three gives the count threshold (three independent sites); AHA gives the decision posture (prefer waiting, prefer clarity over compression, prefer reversibility). Together they explain why mature codebases often look more verbose than the textbook examples that taught their authors: the authors learned to value the cost of mistakes over the cost of repetition. AHA also reframes what counts as a "good" abstraction. A good abstraction is not the one that compresses the most code; it is the one that captures the concern most accurately, has the smallest interface for its responsibilities, and lets the call sites stay honest about what they want. Compression is a side effect of a correct abstraction, not its primary virtue. An auditor reading new code should respect that mature teams sometimes leave deliberate duplication because they are waiting for a third occurrence; the auditor should not flag this as a missed unification without first asking whether the wait is intentional.

**Operational rule:** Default to duplication. Extract only when the shape of the concern has revealed itself across at least three independent uses with consistent intent.

## 4. The Wrong Abstraction

Sandi Metz delivered "All the Little Things" at RailsConf 2014 and condensed its key argument into the 2016 blog post "The Wrong Abstraction". The load-bearing claim of the essay is a single sentence: "Duplication is far cheaper than the wrong abstraction." The argument is empirical, not theoretical. A wrong abstraction accumulates callers, each of which slightly bends the abstraction to fit a case the original designer did not anticipate. Over time the abstraction grows parameters, flags, special cases, and conditional branches, and the cost of changing it grows superlinearly because every change has to consider every caller and every accumulated bend. Duplicated code, by contrast, can be changed one site at a time, and the cost of changing each site is local to that site. A wrong abstraction therefore compounds its cost; duplication does not. The compounding is the load-bearing economic argument: a constant per-site cost of duplication beats a growing cost of a layer that fights its callers.

The escape route Metz prescribes is to re-inline the wrong abstraction back to duplication. This looks like a regression at first; total line count goes up, and the same logic appears in multiple places. But the call sites become honest about what they do, and the real pattern (which was hidden inside the parameter list and conditional branches of the broken abstraction) can be observed in the daylight. From that observed pattern a correct abstraction can be designed later, with a sample size larger than two and with the experience of the failed attempt informing the new shape. The intermediate state of inlined duplication is supposed to look worse; it is supposed to surface the disagreement between the call sites that the abstraction had hidden. Auditors must be willing to recommend this move even when the abstraction has been in the codebase for years and has emotional ownership attached to it. The cost of leaving a wrong abstraction in place compounds; the cost of removing it is paid once. Recommending the inline is the auditor's job. Designing the replacement is the next team's job.

**Operational rule:** When an abstraction is fighting its callers, inline it back to duplication before designing the replacement. The intermediate state is supposed to look worse than both endpoints.

## 5. Locality of Behaviour

Carson Gross introduced "Locality of Behaviour" (LoB) in a 2020 htmx.org essay as the cognitive counter-force to DRY. The principle says that the behaviour of a unit of code should be apparent at the point of definition; a reader should not have to chase the code's effects across multiple files to understand what a call site does. Every unification has a hidden cost: the call site becomes shorter and more abstract, but the reader now has to traverse an additional layer of indirection to recover the actual behaviour. If the unification crosses many files and the traversal is non-trivial, the cognitive cost may outweigh the deduplication benefit. The cost is hidden because it does not show up in line counts, in cyclomatic complexity, or in any metric usually consulted during code review. It shows up later, when a new team member tries to understand a feature and has to open six files to follow a single call.

LoB is not an argument against abstraction. It is an argument against silent abstraction, the kind that hides behaviour behind a clean-looking call site without making the indirection navigable. A unification that is heavily used, well-documented, and has a clear conceptual identity (a money type, an authorization gate, a structured-logger) earns its indirection because the reader stops needing to look inside it once they understand its contract. The contract is stable, the name is well-chosen, the layer is reusable on its own terms. A unification that is lightly used, hand-wavy in its purpose, and demands case-by-case inspection to predict its behaviour does not earn its indirection. The LoB lens reframes the auditor's question. Instead of asking "is this duplicated?" the auditor asks "is this layer earning the cost of its indirection?" If the answer is yes, the layer stays; if no, the layer goes back to the call sites. LoB also penalises abstractions that bridge modules: a unification used by three callers in three different modules has a higher LoB tax than a unification used by three callers in the same file, because the chase is longer.

**Operational rule:** Weigh deduplication against the cognitive cost of chasing definitions across files. If a reader cannot understand a call site without opening N other files, the abstraction has a hidden tax that must be paid back in deduplication value.

## 6. Bounded Contexts

Bounded contexts come from Eric Evans' *Domain-Driven Design* (2003), with Martin Fowler's bliki entry as the most-cited summary. A bounded context is the explicit boundary within which a domain model is consistent. Two contexts may share the name of a concept ("Customer", "Order", "User", "Invoice") but assign different meanings to it. In a billing context, "Customer" carries fiscal identity, payment methods, tax residence, and statutory billing-address requirements. In a CRM context, "Customer" carries contact information, sales history, lead source, and account-ownership relationships. The shared name is a coincidence of natural language, not evidence of a single concept that wants to be unified. The two contexts are answering different questions about the same real-world entity, and the model each context builds is shaped by the question it answers, not by the entity it points at.

The most expensive class of wrong abstraction is the bounded-context fusion: an abstraction that unifies models from two different contexts because they share a name today. Once the fusion is in place, every change in either context has to consider the other. Teams that should be independent become coupled at the model layer. Releases that should be local become cross-team coordination problems. The unification becomes the source of bugs whenever the two contexts evolve in different directions (which they will, because they are answering different questions). The principle is that names that look identical across contexts are signals to *not* unify, not to unify. The seam that separates the contexts is more valuable than the surface similarity that joins them. Auditors must perform an explicit bounded-context check before recommending unification of any cross-module candidate. If the check cannot be performed (the modules' domain ownership is unclear), the finding must be flagged as "context-membership unverified" and downgraded to Medium confidence, because the cost of a wrong unification across a bounded-context boundary is much higher than the cost of duplication within a single context.

**Operational rule:** Two domains may share a name but should not share a model. Bounded contexts mark the seam at which unification is forbidden.

## 7. Tidy First options framing

Kent Beck's *Tidy First?* (2024) reframes abstraction decisions as financial options. An abstraction has an upfront cost (the time to design it, the indirection it adds to call sites, the coupling it creates between previously independent code, the mental overhead it imposes on every future reader). It also has a future value (the cost it saves when the unified concern changes, multiplied by the probability that the change will happen within a relevant time horizon). Both terms must be estimated before the option is exercised. If the upfront cost is high and the probability of the change is low, the option is overpriced and should not be bought, even if the abstraction looks elegant. If the upfront cost is low and the change is likely, the option is cheap and should be bought even if the abstraction looks crude. The framing explicitly treats abstraction as a bet on the future, not as a virtue that justifies itself.

The options framing gives a useful diagnostic for arguing about an abstraction: name the upfront cost in concrete terms (lines added, files added, mental load, coupling created), name the future value in concrete terms (which change becomes cheap, with what probability, in what time frame), and compare. Abstractions defended only on aesthetic grounds (it looks DRYer, it looks cleaner, it is more principled) tend to fail this test, because their upfront cost is real and their future value is hypothetical. Abstractions defended on the basis of an imminent likely change tend to pass it, because both terms are grounded. The options framing also explains the asymmetry between unification and inlining. Buying an abstraction is irreversible at low cost only if you commit early; later, the cost of undoing grows with the number of callers. Selling an abstraction (inlining it back to duplication) is itself an option exercise with its own price: it pays back in clarity and reversibility but costs an outage of working code while the inline happens. Auditors should frame their findings in option-pricing terms when they want a team to act: "the upfront cost of this abstraction is X, the expected future value is Y, and Y < X by a factor of Z; recommendation: inline."

**Operational rule:** Price the abstraction as an option. Upfront cost in coupling, indirection, and cognitive overhead must be less than the expected value of the future change it makes cheaper, discounted by the probability of that change.

## 8. CUPID vs SOLID

Dan North introduced CUPID in "CUPID for joyful coding" (2022) as a successor framing to SOLID. SOLID is a binary checklist of five principles (Single Responsibility, Open/Closed, Liskov Substitution, Interface Segregation, Dependency Inversion); a design either satisfies each principle or it does not, and the principles are taught as object-oriented commandments. CUPID is a set of five continuous properties (Composable, Unix philosophy, Predictable, Idiomatic, Domain-based); a design has more or less of each property, and the properties are evaluated together rather than checked off in sequence. The shift from principles to properties matters for abstraction decisions because real abstractions live on a spectrum, not in a binary state. A layer can be 70% composable and 40% predictable, and that profile may be the right one for its use case. A layer that scores 100% on Open/Closed but is unreadable has passed the SOLID check and failed the actual job.

For the abstraction-vs-duplication question, the CUPID lens is more useful than the SOLID lens because the question is fundamentally about gradients. "Is this layer composable" asks whether other code can build on it; "is it predictable" asks whether a reader can guess its behaviour from its name and signature; "is it idiomatic" asks whether it fits the patterns of the host language and ecosystem; "is it domain-based" asks whether its concepts map cleanly to the business problem. Each property is a dial that can be turned up by improving the layer or down by making it worse. The SOLID question ("does this layer satisfy the Open/Closed Principle?") often yields a yes/no answer that is technically correct but operationally useless, because the cost of the abstraction may be high even when it passes the check. CUPID lets the auditor ask better questions about the layer's lived experience. A finding can recommend CUPID-style improvements ("the auth gate is composable but not predictable: it does N things implicitly that callers do not see; recommendation: narrow its responsibilities to make the dial of predictability go up"), which is more actionable than a SOLID-style verdict ("the auth gate violates the Single Responsibility Principle").

**Operational rule:** Evaluate a layer by its continuous properties (composable, predictable, idiomatic, domain-aligned), not by its conformance to a binary checklist.

## The single rule of thumb

When this concern changes, where do you have to touch?

If the answer is N call sites and N grows linearly with the addition of new features, the concern is a candidate for unification. The unification is paying off because every future feature change touches a single place rather than spreading across the codebase. Money arithmetic, authorization checks, schema validation, retry policies, structured logging, error envelopes, and timezone conversion all tend to behave this way: the concern is real, the duplication is essential, and the unification reduces both the cost of change and the risk of drift. The Rule of Three still applies as a gate, but once it is cleared, the unification is paying its option premium.

If the answer is that every new requirement adds a flag, branch, parameter, or special case to a shared layer, the layer is a wrong abstraction. The growth pattern of a healthy abstraction is "callers use it as-is and the layer rarely changes"; the growth pattern of a wrong abstraction is "every caller pushes another knob onto the layer". When the second pattern appears, the abstraction is fighting its callers, and the escape route is to inline it back to duplication so the real pattern can emerge from a larger sample. The cost of the inline is paid once; the cost of leaving the wrong abstraction in place compounds with every new caller.

This single question subsumes most of what the eight principles above teach. Rule of Three is the count threshold for asking it. DRY is the diagnostic when the answer is "many sites and they all encode the same rule". AHA is the posture that defers the answer until the count is high enough to be confident. The Wrong Abstraction is the diagnostic when the answer is "one layer, but every new feature touches it". Locality of Behaviour is the cognitive correction that asks whether the unification's reader cost is being paid back. Bounded Contexts is the strategic limit on the question itself: do not ask it across contexts. Tidy First is the pricing model that quantifies the answer. CUPID is the language that describes the resulting layer.

When the question is asked honestly and answered with evidence, most abstraction decisions resolve themselves. The auditor's job is to ask the question on the codebase's behalf, gather the evidence (call sites, growth patterns, context membership), and report the answer. The user's job is to act on it.
